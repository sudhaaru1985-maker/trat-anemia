👋 Welcome to the Language of Medicine

Week 1 of medical school — and you're about to learn a new language!

Medicine has its own language — precise, descriptive and universal. A doctor in Kuala Lumpur, Chennai or London who reads “right iliac fossa tenderness” pictures exactly the same thing. Learning this language now will make every lecture, textbook and ward round from here on much easier.

🤯 Did you know? The longest word in English dictionaries is a medical term: pneumonoultramicroscopicsilicovolcanoconiosis (45 letters). Scary? By the end of this module you'll be able to break it down: pneumono lung · ultra beyond · microscopic tiny · silico silica · volcano volcanic dust · coniosis dust disease → a lung disease caused by breathing in very fine volcanic silica dust.

🎯 Learning outcomes

By the end of this TBL, you should be able to:

  1. Explain the structure of medical terms (prefix, root, suffix).
  2. Interpret unfamiliar medical terms using word components.
  3. Use correct anatomical and directional terms to describe the body.
  4. Apply medical terminology to clinical case scenarios.
  5. Appreciate the importance of standardised medical language in communication.

🧩 Structure of Medical Terms

Medical words are like LEGO® — a few blocks snapped together.

Most medical terms are built from three main components:

ComponentMeaningExampleMeaning of example
RootCore meaning (often from Greek or Latin)cardiheart
PrefixComes before the root; modifies its meaningtachy-fast
SuffixComes after the root; tells you the condition, procedure or specialty-itisinflammation

✨ Simple examples

pericarditis
around + heart + inflammation → inflammation of the sac around the heart
bradycardia
slow + heart + condition → a slow heart rate
gastritis
stomach + inflammation → inflammation of the stomach (no prefix needed!)
hepatomegaly
liver + enlargement → an enlarged liver
🔑 Two golden rules 1. Read from the END: start with the suffix, then the prefix, then the root. Pericarditis → “inflammation… around… the heart”.
2. The combining vowel “o”: add o when the suffix starts with a consonant (cardiology, gastroscopy), but drop it when the suffix starts with a vowel (gastr-itis, not “gastroitis”).

🧍 Anatomical Terminology — Basic Orientation

Before “left” and “above” mean anything, everyone must picture the body the same way.

The anatomical position

The body is standing upright, facing forward, feet together, arms at the sides and palms facing forward (thumbs pointing away from the body).

🧠 TipAlways describe structures as if the person is in the anatomical position — even if they are lying in bed, sitting, or upside-down on a gym bar!
↔️ Whose right?“Right” and “left” always mean the patient's right and left — not yours. When you face a patient, their right side is on your left.

🔪 Body Planes and Sections

Imaginary flat surfaces that “slice” the body — exactly how CT and MRI scans show it.

PlaneDivides body intoExample
SagittalRight and left partsA cut straight down through the nose (median/midsagittal = two equal halves)
Coronal (frontal)Front (anterior) and back (posterior)Separates the face from the back of the head
Transverse (axial / horizontal)Upper (superior) and lower (inferior)A cut across the abdomen at the level of the navel
🍞 Think of a loaf of bread
🍞 Transverse = cutting the loaf into slices — like the slices of a CT scan.
👑 Coronal = the line where a crown sits, ear to ear — splits front from back.
🏹 Sagittal = from sagitta (Latin: arrow) — an arrow shot straight through you front-to-back splits left from right.
🏥 In the clinicA CT scan of the abdomen is viewed as transverse (axial) sections — as if you are standing at the patient's feet looking up. That's why the liver appears on the left side of the screen!

🫁 Body Cavities and Regions

The body's “rooms” — and the 9-square map doctors use to describe tummy pain.

Major body cavities

  • Cranial cavity — contains the brain
  • Thoracic cavity — contains the lungs and heart
  • Abdominal cavity — contains the digestive organs
  • Pelvic cavity — contains the urinary bladder and reproductive organs
🧠 TipThe abdominal and pelvic cavities are continuous — together called the abdominopelvic cavity. The diaphragm is the muscular “floor” of the thorax.

🗺️ The nine abdominal regions tap a region

Tap any region to see its name and the organs found there.

Two vertical lines (mid-clavicular) + two horizontal lines (subcostal and transtubercular planes) divide the abdomen like a noughts-and-crosses (tic-tac-toe) grid.
🏥 Why it matters — the wandering pain of appendicitis Early appendicitis often starts as a vague pain in the umbilical region (around the belly button) and then moves to the right iliac region. Just by naming the region, a doctor already narrows down the diagnosis!

🩻 Commonly Used Clinical Terms

Words you'll hear in your very first hospital visit. Tap each card to flip it.

🧠 Symptom vs Sign Symptom = what the patient Says (“I feel dizzy”, “my chest hurts”).
Sign = what the doctor finds — can be seen, felt or measured (fever of 39 °C, a pale tongue, BP 160/100 mmHg).
🔍 Pathology preview Etiology (aetiology) asks “WHY did the disease happen?” — e.g. H. pylori infection causes gastritis.
Pathogenesis asks “HOW did it develop, step by step?” — e.g. bacteria → damage to stomach lining → inflammation → ulcer.
You'll meet these two words in almost every pathology lecture!

🤸 Terms of Movement

Stand up and try each movement as you read — your body is the best textbook!

These terms describe joint and muscle actions and always refer to movement from the anatomical position.

🧠 Mnemonics ABduction — moving Away from the Body · ADDuction — you add the limb back to the body.
Supination — palm up, holding a bowl of soup 🍲 · Pronation — palm down, like lying prone (face down).
Flexion generally moves body parts forward (except at the knee and toes).

🏁 Summary and Practice

You made it! Let's put it all together.

🔑 Key takeaways

  • Medical terms are built systematically from prefixes, roots and suffixes — read them from the end.
  • Anatomical terminology describes location and orientation precisely — always from the anatomical position, using the patient's right and left.
  • Body planes, cavities and abdominal regions give everyone the same “map” of the body.
  • Using the correct term avoids miscommunication — and in medicine, miscommunication can harm patients.
